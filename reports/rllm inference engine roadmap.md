# Cheaper drafts and MoE unlock rllm's next leap

rllm is already at or near the memory-bandwidth wall for single-stream decode on both of its machines. Its single-stream speed, time to first token and low-concurrency serving match or beat llama.cpp, and it beats vLLM at one client. The engine is losing on three fronts. First, **quantized throughput at 16–64 clients** is 0.53–0.60× vLLM on the 8B model. Second, **speculation** relies on a separate draft model, while everyone else has moved to cheap heads that reuse the target's hidden state. Third, **model coverage** stops at three dense families, while users of 128 GB unified-memory boxes have moved to MoE. Your disappointing Strix Halo result follows from physics plus the draft design. Qwen3-8B Q4_K_M reads about 4.67 GB per token, which caps decode near **45–46 tok/s at the ~212–215 GB/s the GPU actually achieves**, so no kernel work will get past the low 40s without speculation. A 0.6B draft costs roughly an eighth of a target step per proposed token on a bandwidth-bound APU, which eats most of the gain. llama.cpp's native MTP, by contrast, reached **1.81–2.44× on Strix Halo**. The biggest practical speedups for you come from two things, in order: a learned draft head (EAGLE-3 for Qwen3-8B, native MTP for Qwen3.5/3.6), and **MoE support**, which turns the Strix Halo's 128 GB into 60–100 tok/s on 30–35B-A3B models. Next come four engineering items: batch-sized and then W4A16 tensor-core GEMMs, a sync-free one-step-ahead scheduler, an llguidance grammar engine, and an honest re-benchmark against llama.cpp's Vulkan backend. Multi-GPU, Vulkan, Metal, NPU and exotic quant formats should wait.

## rllm leads at low concurrency and trails everywhere else

rllm's own measurements set the baseline. On the RTX 6000 Ada it beats llama.cpp by 1.4–1.7× on prompt processing for small models and by 1.5–3.4× under 4–8 concurrent clients. It also delivers the first token 19× sooner at 8 clients, and its draft speculation on Qwen3-8B ran at 213 tok/s, against 126 for llama-server (whose speculation made it slower than no draft at all) (local: `compare.md`, `compared.md`). Against vLLM 0.32 on the same card it leads at one client (147 vs 142 tok/s on 8B 4-bit). At 64 clients it falls to 0.76× on Qwen3-0.6B BF16 and 0.60× on Qwen3-8B Q4_K_M vs AWQ (local: `chart.md`). The weak spots are Qwen3-8B itself, where pp512 on the Ada runs at 0.77× llama.cpp, and AMD decode, which runs at 0.90–0.96× llama.cpp ROCm on 1–8B models.

The competitive map has shifted under rllm in 2026. **vLLM's GGUF support is "highly experimental and under-optimized"** and has moved to an out-of-tree plugin ([vLLM docs](https://docs.vllm.ai/en/latest/features/quantization/gguf/)). SGLang documents first-class GGUF only for Ascend NPUs ([SGLang Ascend quantization](https://docs.sglang.io/docs/hardware-platforms/ascend-npus/ascend_npu_quantization)). Hugging Face put TGI into maintenance mode in December 2025 ([TGI docs](https://huggingface.co/docs/text-generation-inference/index)). None of ExLlamaV3, TurboMind, TensorRT-LLM or mistral.rs targets RDNA3.5, and ExLlamaV3 requires CUDA ([exllamav3](https://github.com/turboderp-org/exllamav3)). A fast, Rust, GGUF-native, concurrency-capable server running on both NVIDIA and AMD is therefore an unoccupied niche, and llama.cpp is the only real incumbent there. That framing should drive priorities: rllm wins by being the best *serving* engine for GGUF, not by matching vLLM's 200 architectures.

Breadth is where the gap is widest. At the commit rllm benchmarks against, llama.cpp lists about 165 architectures and 30-plus quant types, every one of them with an MMQ kernel. It also offers nine speculative modes, including EAGLE-3, MTP and DFlash, plus GBNF/llguidance grammars and nine KV cache types ([llama-arch.h](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/2e0d86b/src/llama-arch.h), [common.h](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/2e0d86b/common/common.h), [mmq.cu](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/2e0d86b/ggml/src/ggml-cuda/mmq.cu)). mistral.rs, the nearest Rust peer, claims 45+ architectures, multimodal input, in-situ quantization of safetensors and FA3. Its vendor numbers, though, show it only at parity to 1.24× llama.cpp on decode, and losing to vLLM at BF16 ([mistral.rs](https://github.com/EricLBuehler/mistral.rs)).

## Strix Halo decode is pinned near 45 tok/s by bandwidth

The Strix Halo's LPDDR5X-8000 bus is rated at 256 GB/s. Community measurements put achievable GPU bandwidth at **about 212–215 GB/s** ([Framework community](https://community.frame.work/t/amd-strix-halo-ryzen-ai-max-395-gpu-llm-performance-tests/72521), [r/LocalLLaMA](https://reddit.birdcat.cafe/r/LocalLLaMA/comments/1kmi3ra/amd_strix_halo_ryzen_ai_max_395_gpu_llm)). Dense 70B decoding at 5.1 tok/s independently confirms a ~205 GB/s practical ceiling ([visorcraft](https://github.com/visorcraft/strix-halo-llm-perf)). Dividing by ~4.67 GB per token gives a practical Qwen3-8B Q4_K_M ceiling of 45–46 tok/s. llama.cpp ROCm's 42.8 sits at about 93% of that, and rllm's 39.3 at about 86%. No published dense 8B Q4_K_M result on any stack exceeds roughly 42–47 tok/s ([Framework community](https://community.frame.work/t/amd-strix-halo-ryzen-ai-max-395-gpu-llm-performance-tests/72521)). Kernel tuning is therefore worth **+10–17% at most**, to about 43–46 tok/s. The clearest target is rllm's fused GLU matvec, which runs at about 177 GB/s while the best kernels reach ~205 GB/s. A cheap experiment is a Vulkan-style variant that dequantizes to f16 and uses FMA instead of dp4a. Vulkan consistently beats HIP on RDNA decode, by roughly 10–30% on MoE models, and no published source explains why at kernel level ([strix-halo-guide](https://github.com/hogeheer499-commits/strix-halo-guide/blob/main/BENCHMARKS.md), [llama.cpp #15021](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/discussions/15021)).

This also means rllm's AMD scoreboard compares against the wrong opponent. Phoronix's October 2026 tests found **llama.cpp Vulkan beating ROCm across models** on two Strix Halo systems ([Phoronix](https://www.phoronix.com/review/lemonade-vulkan-rocm)), so rllm's real decode gap there is likely larger than the 0.92× in `chart.md`. Prefill is a different story. rllm's pp512 of 1333 tok/s already beats every published 8B Q4_K figure found, yet it uses only about 34% of the 59.4 TOPS WMMA peak, while hipBLASLt GEMMs reach 55–62% ([SoftAb](https://whylucian.github.io/softab/docs/hardware-specs.html)). A realistic prefill target is 1.3–1.5×.

### Why the 0.6B draft disappoints, and what fixes it

On a bandwidth-bound APU, drafting is not free. Qwen3-0.6B Q8_0 reads about 12% of the target's bytes per draft token, so proposing five tokens costs roughly 60% of a target step before verification even starts. At ~61% acceptance and temperature near 1, that overhead consumes most of the gain, leaving the +13–30% you saw. EAGLE-3, MTP and DFlash drafters avoid this because they **reuse the target's hidden states and KV cache**, costing about one transformer layer (≈1/36 of the target) per draft token. The evidence on your hardware is direct. llama.cpp's MTP, merged in May 2026, took **Qwen3.6-27B on Strix Halo from 11.7 to 21.2 tok/s at Q4_K_M (1.81×) and from 7.4 to 18.1 at Q8_0 (2.44×)**, with 62–89% acceptance ([SpecPicks](https://specpicks.com/reviews/llama-cpp-mtp-support-landed-qwen3-6-27b-at-2-44-on-a-strix-halo-2-17-on-a-rtx-3090-rig-news)).

For Qwen3-8B specifically, which has no native MTP, the path is EAGLE-3. Converted heads already exist for Qwen3-1.7B through 32B and Llama-3.1-8B ([llama.cpp speculative.md](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/2e0d86b/docs/speculative.md)), and SGLang's SpecForge published "SpecBundle" heads for mainstream models ([SpecForge](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18567)). The EAGLE-3 paper reports **4.44× at temperature 0 and 3.45× at temperature 1 on Llama-3.1-8B**. Its headline numbers rely on draft trees, and chain drafting yields 1.3–1.8× under batching ([arXiv 2503.01840](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3)). DFlash claims about 2.5× more than EAGLE-3 on Qwen3-8B and up to 5× on Qwen3.5-9B at concurrency 1, but no one has reproduced that independently ([arXiv 2602.06036](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.06036), [z-lab card](https://huggingface.co/z-lab/Qwen3.5-9B-DFlash)). With a learned head, a realistic target for Qwen3-8B on Strix Halo is **about 65–90 tok/s on typical chat**, against today's 44–52. That is an inference from the cited speedups applied to rllm's 39 tok/s base, discounted for chain drafting and temperature.

## The concurrency gap is a GEMM problem first, then host overhead

The 8B serving collapse has a measured signature. Throughput is 905 tok/s at 8 sequences and drops to 610 at 9, where decode leaves the matvec for MMQ's fixed 64-token tile (local: `compare.md`). llama.cpp instead picks its MMQ token-tile width from 8 to 128 in steps of 8, choosing the width with the fewest column tiles that fits shared memory. For Q4_K on Ada it uses 128-row tiles with stream-K ([mmq.cu](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/2e0d86b/ggml/src/ggml-cuda/mmq.cu), [mmq-config-ampere.cuh](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/2e0d86b/ggml/src/ggml-cuda/mmq-config-ampere.cuh)). Porting that dispatch rule is cheap, because NVRTC/hiprtc compiles only the widths actually used. The same change likely explains the **Qwen3-8B pp512 deficit on the Ada**: at 512 tokens llama.cpp runs a 128×128 tile, while rllm's 128×64 reads each weight tile twice as often.

Batch-sized tiles recover part of the gap. vLLM's remaining edge comes from its kernel class. Marlin-style W4A16 kernels keep activations in FP16 and need no int8 quantization pass. They stream pre-shuffled 4-bit weights through a 4-stage `cp.async` pipeline and pad M only to 16. They hold near the ideal 4-bit speedup up to batch 16–32 (2.30× over FP16 at batch 16 on an RTX 3090 end to end) ([MARLIN](https://arxiv.org/html/2408.11743v1)). LMDeploy's TurboMind goes further. It packs weights offline into an MMA-ready layout and overlaps integer-to-float dequantization with tensor-core work. The result **matches FP16 GEMM at batch 64, where Marlin loses up to 20%**, and beats vLLM+Marlin by 13% on average end to end ([arXiv 2508.15601](https://arxiv.org/html/2508.15601v1)). GGUF on disk stays untouched. rllm can repack Q4_K/Q5_K/Q6_K/IQ4_XS at load time into such a layout, with scales unpacked to FP16 per 32-weight group. This is the same transformation vLLM applies to AWQ checkpoints, and it is the most plausible route to parity with vLLM at 16–64 clients.

The 0.6B BF16 decline from 0.96× to 0.76× has a different cause, since its GEMM is plain BF16. It points to per-step host work and syncs. Both leaders have eliminated these. vLLM's Model Runner V2 builds step inputs on the GPU from a persistent per-request state table and consumes rejection-sampling results on the device. It reported **+56% on Qwen3-0.6B (16K → 25K tok/s on GB200)** purely from runner work ([vLLM MRV2](https://vllm.ai/blog/mrv2)). SGLang's Spec V2 overlaps host cleanup and KV allocation with the GPU's previous batch. That took **Qwen3-8B on B200 at concurrency 32 from ~11.4k to ~15.3k tok/s (+33%)** ([LMSYS](https://lmsys.org/blog/2026-06-15-next-generation-speculative-decoding-dflash-v2/)). rllm has no Python interpreter to fight, so its remaining overhead is syncs. A one-step-ahead pipeline is the fix: schedule step N+1 during step N, read sampled tokens back a step late from pinned memory, and let stop checks lag one token. It also makes grammar masks and speculation free to overlap, and it lets speculation stay on at higher batch. EAGLE-3 still gave **1.38× at batch 64 in SGLang**, whereas rllm currently stops speculating at five decoders ([arXiv 2503.01840](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01840v3)).

## Coverage should follow MoE, because Strix Halo users already have

The download data says rllm's dense set covers the small end well. Qwen3-0.6B and Qwen3-8B are the #1 and #3 most-downloaded LLMs. But **Qwen3-VL-8B (#2), Qwen3-Coder-30B-A3B GGUF, Qwen3.6-35B-A3B, Gemma-4-26B-A4B and Qwen3.5-9B** all sit in the top ten ([Digital Applied HF snapshot](https://www.digitalapplied.com/blog/most-downloaded-open-ai-models-hugging-face-september-2026)). On the Strix Halo, the community runs Qwen3-30B-A3B IQ4_XS at **~100 tok/s decode** and gpt-oss-120b MXFP4 at **~56 tok/s**, against ~5 tok/s for dense 70B ([strix-halo-guide](https://github.com/hogeheer499-commits/strix-halo-guide/blob/main/BENCHMARKS.md)). MoE decode costs about as much as a dense model the size of the active parameters, and a 128 GB pool holds the whole model. For a Strix Halo owner, MoE is the single largest *perceived* speed upgrade available: better models at 2–2.5× the tok/s of Qwen3-8B.

The build order follows dependencies. **Qwen2/2.5** needs only attention biases. **MoE** needs a top-k router (softmax or sigmoid), a shared expert, a gather-matvec for decode and an expert-sorted grouped MMQ for prefill. A dedicated gathered quantized matmul gave a 2.1× gain in one Qwen3.6 port ([HF model card](https://huggingface.co/mlboydaisuke/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B-CoreAI)). That unlocks Qwen3-30B-A3B, Qwen3-Coder and Gemma-4-26B-A4B. **gpt-oss** then adds MXFP4 experts and learned attention sinks over rllm's existing SWA, with a strict mask boundary that Unsloth found the reference requires ([Unsloth](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/gpt-oss-how-to-run-and-fine-tune/long-context-gpt-oss-training)). **Qwen3.5/3.6/3.8** is the costliest and most valuable step. Their layers are 3:1 Gated DeltaNet to gated attention, over 256-expert MoE, with native MTP ([vLLM recipe](https://recipes.vllm.ai/Qwen/Qwen3.6-35B-A3B?variant=default)). The engine needs a chunked scan for prefill, a recurrent update for decode, and per-draft-position state snapshots so speculation can roll back. llama.cpp needed a separate PR before MTP worked on hybrid models at all ([community report](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1t65vl8/snapshots/2026-05-09T00%3A46%3A53.937033Z)). Prefix caching for recurrent state requires checkpoints rather than paged blocks. Vision (Qwen3-VL ViT with M-RoPE) follows. DeepSeek V4 and Kimi-class models are too large to matter on a single GPU.

Speculation on MoE pays less than on dense, because each draft token touches different experts. DFlash reports up to 2.8× on Qwen3.5-35B-A3B against 5.0× on dense Qwen3.5-9B ([z-lab 35B card](https://huggingface.co/z-lab/Qwen3.5-35B-A3B-DFlash)). rllm's adaptive draft length should account for the size of the expert union.

For quant types, coverage matters more than novelty. The order is Q4_0/Q5_0/Q5_1, then Q3_K/Q2_K for low-bit mixes, then MXFP4, which gpt-oss ships exclusively, then the IQ2/IQ3 codebooks. llama.cpp itself falls back to dequantize-plus-BLAS for Q2_K and Q6_K at large batch on RDNA3 ([mmq.cu](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/2e0d86b/ggml/src/ggml-cuda/mmq.cu)), so new types can ship with a dp4a matvec plus a BLAS prefill path and gain MMQ later. EXL3 does beat Q4_K_M on KL divergence per bit (0.0248 vs 0.0399), but it decoded **15–21% slower than llama.cpp** in the one controlled test ([Atomic Chat](https://atomic.chat/blog/guides/exl3-vs-gguf)). On a bandwidth-optimized engine like rllm that would be a net loss. ik_llama.cpp's exclusive formats depend on a single-author, CUDA-only ecosystem ([ik_llama.cpp](https://github.com/ikawrakow/ik_llama.cpp)), so skip both.

## Prioritized roadmap

The order front-loads cheap, measured fixes (#1, #2). It then puts the item that answers your Strix Halo complaint (#3) ahead of the larger serving refactor (#4), because #3 delivers user-visible speed on the hardware you own. #4 must precede #6, #7 and batched speculation, since all three depend on overlapping host and device work. MoE (#5) comes before hybrid layers (#10) because every hybrid model in demand is also MoE, so #10 builds on #5. Two items are deliberately absent from the near term. Tensor parallelism has no hardware behind it, and llama.cpp's own `tensor` split is still experimental, so rllm loses nothing by waiting ([multi-gpu.md](https://github.com/ggml-org/llama.cpp/blob/2e0d86b/docs/multi-gpu.md)). The NPU prefilled gpt-oss-20b at ~97–413 tok/s against the iGPU's ~1643 ([r/LocalLLaMA snapshot](https://reddit.sentinel-team.org/posts/1rj3i8m/snapshots/2026-03-04T15%3A10%3A50.627029Z)), so it is not worth targeting.

Several numbers remain uncertain. No one has published vLLM, SGLang or LMDeploy concurrency sweeps on an RTX 6000 Ada, so the "~1.0× vLLM" target for #6 extrapolates from A100/4090-class papers. DFlash's headline speedups have not been independently reproduced. The causes of Vulkan's decode edge on RDNA are hypotheses that rocprof should test before #9 commits to a design. The Strix Halo bandwidth ceiling also rests on forum measurements, not vendor figures.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question rllm should be asking. It is no longer "how do we beat llama.cpp's kernels?", because on dense single-stream decode rllm is within a few percent of what the hardware allows on both boxes. It is now "how do we read fewer bytes per useful token?" Learned draft heads, MoE and suffix speculation all answer that question, and none of them is a kernel race. On the Strix Halo the honest ceiling for Qwen3-8B without speculation is mid-40s tok/s. The practical way to roughly double perceived speed is a cheap draft head on the same model, or a move to a 30–35B-A3B MoE that rllm cannot yet load.

The strategic opening is also clearer than `compared.md` implies. vLLM and SGLang have effectively abandoned GGUF, TGI is archived, and no CUDA-first engine runs on RDNA3.5. A GGUF server that matches vLLM at 16–64 clients, ships EAGLE-3 and MTP, and loads the 2026 MoE families on both NVIDIA and AMD would have no direct competitor except llama-server. rllm already beats llama-server under load by 1.5–3.4×. Roadmap items #1–#6 close the gaps that keep that position from being decisive.
